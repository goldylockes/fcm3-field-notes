Introduction

Medicine does not happen in isolation.

Family and Community Medicine asks us to see patients beyond disease alone—to understand the families, communities, environments, and systems that shape health.


This journal follows that perspective across each week of FCM 3, from revisiting the foundations of primary health care to returning to the community itself.

About the Author

Karen Isabelle F. Fernando

Year 3 · Pamantasan ng Lungsod ng Maynila

I am a Year 3 medical student at Pamantasan ng Lungsod ng Maynila, documenting my experiences in Family and Community Medicine 3 through this weekly reflective journal. These entries trace how my understanding of health continues to develop beyond individual disease—toward the families, communities, environments, and systems that shape the lives of patients.

Medical school has taught me to pay attention to details, but FCM has challenged me to ask what those details mean outside the hospital or classroom. I am learning that a diagnosis can explain only part of a person's health story; the rest may be shaped by work, family, access, environment, health knowledge, and the everyday decisions people have to make.

I am still figuring out the kind of physician I want to become, but I know I want to be someone who can combine careful clinical thinking with an understanding of the person and community behind the case. I value being observant, asking questions, and revisiting my first interpretation when new information changes the picture.

This portfolio is therefore both a course requirement and a record of that process. It gives me a place to look back on what I noticed, what surprised me, what I misunderstood, and what I want to carry forward as my medical training continues.

Foundations · July 22, 2026

Returning to the Bigger Picture

Family, social determinants, and the many roles of a physician.

Week 1 of FCM 3 was a recap of key concepts from FCM 1 and FCM 2 to ease us back into the course. We revisited how families respond to health and illness—the different ways a family unit adjusts, copes, and reorganizes itself when a member gets sick, and how that response can either support or hinder recovery.

We also went over the WHO's 6-star doctor concept, which outlines the six roles a physician is expected to fulfill. Tying into that, we touched on social determinants of health again, reinforcing the idea that a doctor's role extends beyond the clinic and into addressing the broader conditions that shape a patient's health.

Patients are not simply individuals with a diagnosis, but people embedded in families and communities.

It was a useful reset before starting a new year in the course, especially since these frameworks tie directly into how we're expected to think about patients this term—not just as individuals with a diagnosis, but as people embedded in families and communities.

Revisiting the 6-star doctor model in particular made me reflect on how much of good primary care depends on roles we don't always associate with “being a doctor,” like being a manager or community leader. It set the stage well for the deeper dive into social determinants of health and primary health care that followed in the next sessions.

Seeing patients through family, social, and community contexts changes what good care requires.

The physician roles that stood out most were the ones outside the usual clinical image—especially manager and community leader.

Context · July 29, 2026

The Patient Beyond the Diagnosis

Seeing chronic disease through the circumstances that shape it.
Hypothetical teaching case · no real patient information is presented.

For Week 2, we were grouped and assigned a hypothetical teaching case to report on and discuss in relation to social determinants of health and primary health care.

The fictional case involved “Mr. Santos,” a 58-year-old tricycle driver with poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and recurrent diabetic foot infections. As a group, we broke down how his occupation and everyday circumstances tied into his poor disease control.

The physically demanding, irregular nature of tricycle driving made consistent follow-up and medication adherence difficult, and this was compounded by limited access to healthcare services, inadequate patient education about managing his conditions, and poor coordination between health services and social support systems.

Disease does not exist separately from the life in which it occurs.

In discussing possible solutions, we focused on improving healthcare access and strengthening continuity of care through primary health care as the key levers for change.

We reasoned that if Mr. Santos had more consistent access to a primary care provider who could coordinate his diabetes, hypertension, and kidney disease management in one continuous relationship, many of his complications—like the recurrent foot infections—could be caught and managed before they escalated to the point of hospitalization.

It reinforced for me how a strong primary health care system isn't just about treating illness but about addressing the social and structural barriers that make illness worse in the first place.

D
Discovery

Continuity of primary care can prevent complications by addressing medical needs together with the barriers that make disease harder to control.

S
Surprise

A patient's occupation and daily schedule can influence follow-up, medication adherence, and ultimately clinical outcomes in very concrete ways.

A
Affirmation

The case reinforced the importance of social determinants of health in understanding why chronic disease may remain poorly controlled.

C
Challenge

The challenge is designing care that is medically sound while still being realistic for a patient's work, access, resources, and everyday circumstances.

Field Note · 02

Primary Care · August 03, 2026

Health for All, Then and Now

From Alma-Ata to Astana—and why primary health care still matters.

This week's session picked up from our earlier introduction to social determinants of health and tied it more closely to primary health care and universal health coverage.

We talked about how factors such as income, education, housing, employment, and access to services shape a person's health outcomes just as much as, if not more than, clinical care itself. Framing primary health care and universal health coverage within that context made it clearer why addressing health can't stop at the level of the individual patient.

1978 Alma-Ata Declaration
→
2018 Astana Declaration

We went through the Alma-Ata Declaration of 1978, the first major international commitment to primary health care as a strategy for achieving “Health for All,” which emphasized community participation, equity, and intersectoral collaboration.

From there we discussed the Astana Declaration of 2018, which reaffirmed and updated those same principles for the modern context, with a stronger push toward integrated health services and empowering people and communities to take an active role in their own health.

Forty years apart, the language changed—but the foundation remained remarkably familiar.

Seeing how two declarations 40 years apart still echo the same core values made me appreciate how foundational the primary health care approach really is, and how relevant it still is to the health issues we're learning about now.

D
Discovery

Alma-Ata and Astana, despite being 40 years apart, are anchored in many of the same primary health care principles.

S
Surprise

The continuity of ideas such as equity, participation, and accessible care across decades was more striking than I expected.

A
Affirmation

The discussion reaffirmed that strong primary health care remains central to improving health beyond the level of individual treatment.

C
Challenge

The challenge is translating broad international principles into practical action within actual local communities and health systems.

Field Note · 03

Reassessment · August 12, 2026

Looking Back Before Moving Forward

Revisiting what we once knew about the community.

Week 4 was centered on revisiting our community diagnosis, starting with a presentation of the collated paper from last year's data.

Our group went through the past findings together, which centered on three major health concerns in the community: diarrheal diseases, upper respiratory tract infections, and non-communicable diseases, particularly hypertension and diabetes mellitus.

Before deciding what needs to change, we first have to ask whether what we once knew still holds true.

Going through last year's paper as a group helped us get everyone on the same page, since some of us remembered different details from when the data was originally gathered.

Presenting it back to each other also made it easier to spot which parts of the data might already be outdated or need rechecking once we go back to collect updated information.

It felt like a necessary first step before we could move on to re-identifying and re-prioritizing the community's health problems in the coming weeks—we needed to be reminded of exactly what we'd found before deciding what still holds true and what needs revising.

D
Discovery

A community diagnosis is not permanent; it has to be reassessed as conditions, memories, and available information change.

S
Surprise

Even within our own group, people remembered different details from the earlier data collection, showing how easily a shared picture can become uneven over time.

A
Affirmation

Reviewing the earlier diagnosis before moving forward affirmed the value of reassessment rather than assuming old findings still apply.

C
Challenge

The challenge is identifying which findings remain valid and which need updated community data before reprioritizing problems.

Field Note · 04

Community diagnosis is not static. Good assessment begins by questioning whether yesterday's picture still describes today's community.

Root Causes · August 26, 2026

Tracing the Roots

Understanding the causes beneath the problems we see.

For Week 6, our main focus was constructing the problem tree based on the five priority problems we finalized the week before.

We worked through mapping out the root causes, core problems, and effects for each, laying them out in a way that showed how underlying factors like health knowledge deficit and poor environmental conditions connected to and likely worsened the disease-related problems.

Reading a problem from the roots upward.

Roots Contributing and underlying factors
Trunk The central community health problem
Branches Consequences and downstream effects
The most visible problem is not necessarily where an intervention should begin.

Building the problem tree made the relationships between causes and effects much more concrete than just listing the problems on their own.

It pushed us to think more critically about what was actually driving these conditions in the community, rather than treating each problem as isolated, and gave us a clearer framework to work from once we collect the new data and move into planning actual interventions.

Discovery

A problem tree makes the relationship between root causes, a central problem, and its downstream effects much easier to see.

Partnership · September 02, 2026

Before We Enter the Community

Why community work begins with relationships.
Barangay courtesy visit · September 2026

Week 7 was originally scheduled to be our community visit, but it ended up being more limited than planned. Instead of going out into the community itself, we went to the barangay hall for a courtesy visit to the barangay officials.

It still felt like an important step, since building rapport with the officials is essential before we can actually go around collecting data or interacting with residents. We used the time to introduce our group and our project, and to formally inform them about our plans for the community diagnosis this year.

Community work begins before data collection—it begins with trust.

Even though it wasn't the full community immersion we expected, the courtesy visit served as a necessary formality to get local officials' buy-in and, hopefully, their support once we do proceed with actual data collection.

It was a good reminder that community work often has to go through these institutional channels first—you can't just show up and start collecting data without first establishing that relationship with the people who represent and know the area best.

Hopefully this paves the way for a smoother, more substantial community visit in the coming weeks.

Discovery

Community engagement starts with relationships and local coordination before formal data collection begins.

Adaptation · September 09, 2026

When the Weather Changed the Plan

Fieldwork, flexibility, and making use of an interrupted day.

Week 8 was supposed to be another community visit to collect additional data, but heavy rain got in the way. I managed to get to the university, but by the time I arrived I was completely soaked from the downpour, so I decided to head back home instead of continuing on.

As it turned out, the rest of the class who stayed and made it to the classroom had gone through the same flooded streets I did, and the professor ultimately decided not to send anyone out to the community given the conditions.

Not every productive fieldwork day has to happen in the field.

Instead, we had an online class for the rest of the period, using the time to revise our community diagnosis presentation. In a way, it worked out—even though we didn't get the additional data we were hoping to collect, we made good use of the time by refining what we already had.

It was a practical adjustment to an uncooperative situation, and a reminder that fieldwork often has to bend around things completely outside our control, like the weather.

Discovery

Fieldwork requires flexibility because safety and external conditions can change a plan completely.

S
Surprise

Fieldwork · September 23, 2026

From Doorsteps to Data

Collecting, digitizing, and preparing community data for analysis.
Pre-fieldwork briefing at PLM · Group assignments and last-minute instructions · September 23, 2026

Week 9 was dedicated to collecting additional data for our community diagnosis in Plastikan Compound, Brgy. 655. We started early at 9 AM under sunny, humid weather, working in pairs and interviewing residents to cover more ground efficiently.

The residents were cooperative, which made data collection go smoothly, and we managed to finish by 10 AM, well within our allotted time. Compared with Week 8's rained-out attempt, it was a relief to finally complete the additional fieldwork we had been waiting to do.

Collecting data is only the first step; organizing it is what makes it usable.

In the afternoon, we shifted to encoding the responses we'd gathered through face-to-face interviews into Google Forms so that everyone's individual data could be collated into a single Google Sheets file. This made it much easier to consolidate our group's data collection and prepare it for the next stage of analysis.

Having the additional data both collected and digitized by the end of the day made the progress feel concrete. After the interruption of the previous community visit, we were finally in a good position to move forward with updating our community diagnosis.

D
Discovery

Efficient fieldwork depends not only on interviewing residents, but also on teamwork, respondent cooperation, and a clear system for turning individual responses into a usable dataset.

S
Surprise

Despite the sunny, humid conditions, working in pairs and meeting cooperative residents allowed us to finish the additional data collection within only an hour.

A
Affirmation

The encoding process affirmed that organizing and consolidating information is an essential part of community diagnosis, not merely an administrative step after fieldwork.

C
Challenge

Community Validation · October 08, 2026

Closing the Loop

Presenting the community diagnosis back to the people it describes.
Community diagnosis presentation and validation · Barangay 655 · October 08, 2026

Week 10 felt like the culmination of our community diagnosis so far. We returned to the barangay hall to present our findings in front of barangay officials and members of the community. The weather was sunny and humid at first and later turned into a brief drizzle, but we were still able to complete the presentation without any major interruptions.

What stood out most was the discussion that followed. Rather than simply presenting our findings to the community, the session became genuinely two-way. The barangay officials and community members shared their own perspectives on the health problems we had identified, giving us the opportunity to compare our interpretation of the data with the experiences of the people actually living in the community.

A community diagnosis should not only describe a community—it should also make sense to the people who live there.

Hearing their inputs made the findings feel more grounded. It was also a useful check on whether our interpretation actually matched their experiences. The discussion reminded me that community diagnosis is not complete once the data have been analyzed or the presentation has been prepared; returning the findings to the community creates space for validation, clarification, and perspectives that numbers alone may not capture.

Overall, it was a fruitful and pleasant session and a rewarding way to close the loop after weeks of reassessment, prioritization, fieldwork, data collection, encoding, and preparation.

D
Discovery

Presenting findings back to the community is an important part of community diagnosis because it allows our interpretation to be checked against lived experience.

S
Surprise

The discussion became much more interactive than a simple presentation, with community members actively contributing their own perspectives on the problems we identified.

Affirmation

Challenge

The challenge is remaining open to feedback and recognizing that our analysis may need refinement when community perspectives reveal context that the collected data cannot fully show.

Program Planning · October 14, 2026

From Diagnosis to Design

Turning community priorities into a plan for action.

Week 11 marked a shift from describing community health problems toward thinking about how interventions can actually be planned. The lecture introduced several tools for organizing and analyzing community health programs, including the 4×4 logical framework, the IPOO (input-process-output-outcome) framework, SWOT analysis, and stakeholder analysis, along with other planning tools discussed during the session.

Seeing the frameworks together made the transition from community diagnosis to program planning much clearer. Up to this point, much of our work had focused on identifying, prioritizing, and validating problems. These tools added the next question: once a priority has been established, how do we systematically think through an intervention and the factors that may help or hinder it?

Identifying a community problem is only the beginning; planning asks what can realistically be done about it.

After the lecture, we were divided into four groups to apply the planning tools to our own community diagnosis. Our group was assigned the SWOT analysis for acute respiratory illnesses, the top priority problem we had identified in the community. Working directly on our number one priority made the framework feel much more practical than it had during the lecture.

We had to consider what the community already has going for it, what gaps or barriers exist, and what external opportunities or threats could affect a future intervention. It felt like a good first step into the planning phase of the course because we were no longer only describing the problem; we were beginning to organize what would matter when deciding what to do next.

D
Discovery

Planning frameworks help bridge the gap between identifying a community problem and designing an intervention around it.

S
Surprise

The frameworks felt much more useful once we applied one to a priority problem from our own community diagnosis instead of viewing it only as a lecture concept.

A
Affirmation

The activity affirmed that prioritizing a problem is not the endpoint; good community work also requires a structured way to think about what comes next.

C
Challenge

The challenge was separating the factors in the SWOT analysis clearly without oversimplifying how strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats can interact.

Field Note · 11

Intervention Planning · October 21, 2026

Designing for Daycare

Translating ARI priorities into a concrete plan for young children and their teachers.

For Week 12, all four groups presented our revised IPOO, SWOT, and stakeholder analyses for the community diagnosis, building on the planning frameworks introduced in the previous lecture. Seeing the three tools side by side made their relationship much clearer: the SWOT and stakeholder analysis helped us identify what the community already has, what barriers we need to account for, and who we can work with, while the IPOO framework laid out how inputs and processes can lead to the outputs and outcomes we want.

After the presentations, we were asked to propose activity plans for different target groups. Our group, Group 4, was assigned the daycare children ages 3 to 5 and their teachers, or Child Development Workers (CDWs), in Barangay 655. We chose them because toddlers have developing immune systems, often put their hands in their mouths, and stay in close contact with one another every day, which makes the daycare a major transmission hub for acute respiratory illnesses (ARIs). The teachers were just as important because they are the adults who observe the children daily and can reinforce preventive practices consistently.

Planning becomes real when an intervention is specific enough to say who it is for, what should change, and how that change will be measured.

Our plan aimed to improve the knowledge, attitudes, and hygiene practices of both groups over a three-month period from December to February. We proposed interactive storytelling about ARIs and hygiene, a teaching and return demonstration on handwashing, IEC materials such as posters, a video, and a jingle, and the distribution of hygiene materials. Because the main audience included very young children, we had to keep the activities simple, visual, and hands-on rather than purely lecture-based.

We also made the objectives measurable. For the children, we proposed at least a 20-percentage-point increase in knowledge scores and at least 90% correctly demonstrating the 7-step handwashing technique. For the teachers, we included a focus group discussion to identify gaps in daily hygiene practices and to develop a written action plan together. Involving the CDWs in this way reflected the community participation emphasized in primary health care, while the whole activity plan connected directly back to the health knowledge deficit we had identified earlier in the course.

D
Discovery

IPOO, SWOT, and stakeholder analysis are most useful when read together because each one answers a different part of how an intervention can actually work.

S
Surprise

Designing for toddlers made planning feel much more concrete; every activity had to be simplified enough to be engaging without losing its health purpose.

A
Affirmation

The activity reaffirmed that prevention works better when the people who interact with the target group daily—in this case the CDWs—are included as partners rather than as bystanders.

C
Challenge

The challenge was translating a broad priority problem into age-appropriate, measurable activities that are realistic to carry out over only three months.

Field Note · 12

A good community plan becomes tangible when it identifies a target group, builds with the people around them, and defines change in terms that can actually be observed.
